# Independent review: the precise delayed-reset task and comparison

This is a scope-specific mathematical review of the R18 continuous task used by
the R19 derivative study. It supplies an independent comparison argument for
the declared synthetic task. It does not identify that task with the full
He–Xiong model, prove its continuum free-boundary regularity, or turn finite
numerical checks into a certified convergence rate.

## Exact task

The public state is an observed mode `j∈{0,1}` and `x∈[−4,4]`. While waiting,
`x` follows normally reflected Brownian motion with constant `σ>0`; the mode
does not change. STOP produces a Bernoulli success whose conditional mean is
the declared bounded continuous payoff `g_j(x)`. Maximizing its expected reward
is therefore exactly the payoff control problem with terminal reward `g`.
The future Bernoulli draw is unavailable to the actor.

WAIT costs `c_wait` per elapsed unit. RESET incurs `k_j+c_rec ρ`, consumes a
fixed strictly positive time `ρ`, and admits no intervening decision. At
completion it sets `x=x_reset∈[−4,4]` and samples the observed next mode from
the stochastic row `M_j`. A reset is legal if it completes by the deadline,
including equality. On completion all subsequent decisions use the remaining
time. STOP is always available; STOP at the deadline is mandatory.

Admissible policies are nonanticipating stopping/intervention strategies for
this public process, with independent transition/reward randomness. Their
number of completed resets is at most `floor(t/ρ)` from remaining time `t`.
There is consequently no instantaneous intervention loop or infinite-reset
limit. For the registered nonnegative costs and `0≤g≤1`, immediate STOP and
the reward bound give `0≤V≤1`.

With remaining time as the forward PDE variable, the reset value is

`B_j(t)=−k_j−c_recρ+Σ_l M_jl V_l(t−ρ,x_reset)`, for `t≥ρ`.

It is unavailable at `t<ρ`. This formula retains the successor mode, future
resets, and all remaining time. Replacing the successor value by the immediate
payoff would define a different task.

## Causal comparison, without a simultaneous nonlocal fixed point

Positive `ρ` permits induction over slabs `(kρ,(k+1)ρ)`. Once earlier slabs
are known, the function `B` on the next open slab is known. In that slab each
mode is an ordinary scalar optimal-stopping problem with continuous obstacle
`h_j(t,x)=max(g_j(x),B_j(t))`, or just `g_j` before the first admission, and
known initial data at `kρ`. Its interior variational inequality is

`min(V_j−h_j, ∂tV_j−(σ²/2)∂xxV_j+c_wait)=0`.

For this one-dimensional constant-coefficient task there is an especially
direct way to treat reflection. Extend every spatial function by the fold map
from the circle of length16 onto `[−4,4]`. The lift is even at each endpoint;
folding ordinary Brownian motion on the circle gives the reflected process.
The lifted payoff and initial values are continuous, even if their derivatives
have a cusp at a fold. Scalar parabolic obstacle comparison on the circle
then gives comparison for the reflected stopping problem. This avoids
assuming the immediate-exercise payoff has a classical zero endpoint slope.
Equivalently one may use relaxed viscosity Neumann conditions on the interval.

Here is the delayed-system induction. Suppose a bounded piecewise-continuous
subsolution `u` and supersolution `v` have ordered initial values, satisfy the
scalar obstacle inequalities in each open slab, and satisfy the ordered
admission inequalities below. In the first slab scalar comparison yields
`u≤v`. If it holds on all preceding slabs, `M≥0` implies `B[u]≤B[v]` on the
next slab, including their endpoint limits. Scalar obstacle comparison with
ordered obstacles and initial data again gives `u≤v`. The admission maximum
is order preserving, so the order also survives the next endpoint. A finite
induction reaches any finite registered horizon. Applying this twice gives
uniqueness in this piecewise-continuous, right-valued admission class.

Existence is constructed by the same induction using the reflected-process
optimal-stopping/Snell value in each slab, with the previous right-valued
boundary as the terminal continuation value. The compact state space,
continuous bounded data, nonnegative stochastic `M`, and positive delay are
the assumptions used here. A full arbitrary state-dependent or zero-delay
impulse-control theorem is unnecessary and is not asserted.

## The admission condition is part of the problem

At `t=kρ`, the value must satisfy

`V_j(kρ,x)=max(V_j(kρ−,x), B_j(kρ))`.

The left limit is generated by strategies with fewer than `k` feasible
resets. Any strictly positive initial waiting time moves the remaining horizon
below `kρ`, so it cannot access the new exact-count reset opportunity. An
immediate RESET accesses `B_j(kρ)`; immediate STOP is already included in the
left limit. Randomizing these initial decisions cannot exceed their maximum.
Continuity of the waiting process and the continuous spatial left-limit value
give the converse limiting argument for zero waiting durations.

At the first admission, the left obstacle has no RESET. At later admissions,
the left reset value uses `V((k−1)ρ−,x_reset)`, whereas the new right reset
value uses `V((k−1)ρ,x_reset)`. Thus a global viscosity equation with no
explicit admission convention would underspecify this task. A globally
continuous value is not generally justified. Time differentiability at these
dates, or ordinary derivatives with respect to a delay that moves their
locations, do not follow from the slab comparison argument.

The actual `jump_solver.py` follows this order: solve the implicit LCP with
the old/left reset obstacle; retain its left-limit array; then take the
pointwise maximum with the new/right reset obstacle. Its integer lag is
validated on the fixed time lattice. The original run01 solver instead
previewed the newly available reset during the preceding implicit step. That
earlier source and failed outcomes remain separate. R19 derivatives capture
the repaired solver's actual returned primal and left-limit arrays; they do
not silently use the old smooth-time map.

## What the numerical evidence establishes

The reflected finite-difference generator has nonnegative off-diagonal rates
and zero row sums. Its implicit matrix is an M-matrix. Linear reset
interpolation inside the domain, stochastic `M`, scalar obstacle maximization,
and the admission maximum preserve order. These are the relevant finite-map
monotonicity ingredients. They also explain why active-branch Jacobians are
linear solves and why a branch switch requires a directional critical-cone
contract rather than an ordinary Jacobian.

The stored residuals, positive-reference agreement, and mesh refinements are
finite numerical checks. A full convergence proof would additionally state
and verify consistency at reflecting folds and admission interfaces, stability,
aligned-delay refinement, and the appropriate half-relaxed-limit arguments
slab by slab. The general monotone-scheme convergence framework is useful for
that proof; citing it alone would not complete these task-specific steps.
No certified continuous boundary brackets, global smooth fit, or rate follows
merely from the recorded mesh gaps.

Actual owner execution uses exact folded-Gaussian endpoints on the registered
decision mesh. It does not sample the continuous first hitting time of an
exercise boundary. Therefore owner returns qualify that decision-mesh policy;
comparison of the underlying continuum value is a separate mathematical
statement. The finite15-coordinate derivative contract fixes the domain,
grid, horizon and `ρ`, and excludes topology/admission/ordinary-action ties.

## Primary references and their role

These references supply general background, not a claim that their theorems
already certify the repository's complete discontinuous-delay implementation.

- Crandall, Ishii and Lions (1992), *User's guide to viscosity solutions of
  second order partial differential equations*,
  [arXiv:math/9207212](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/9207212),
  [DOI10.1090/S0273-0979-1992-00266-5](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0273-0979-1992-00266-5).
  Example1.7 treats obstacle operators, §7 explains generalized boundary
  conditions, and §8 treats parabolic comparison. The slab induction and fold
  reduction above are this review's application to the declared task; Theorem8.2
  by itself is not a ready-made Neumann/delayed-jump theorem.
- Lions and Sznitman (1984), *Stochastic differential equations with reflecting
  boundary conditions*,
  [DOI10.1002/cpa.3160370408](https://doi.org/10.1002/cpa.3160370408).
  This is a primary reference for reflected diffusion construction; the present
  constant-coefficient interval additionally has the explicit fold construction.
- Barles and Souganidis (1991), *Convergence of approximation schemes for fully
  nonlinear second order equations*,
  [DOI10.3233/ASY-1991-4305](https://doi.org/10.3233/ASY-1991-4305).
  Its monotonicity/stability/consistency/comparison framework motivates the
  separate convergence obligations above. It is not a measured error bound.
